    Christian Mackin · Orientation for engineers · 60 minutes

    Define what matters. Choose the tools and data boundaries. Give it context. Delegate the work — keep human review and accountability.

    The flip: AI proposes the how.

    The how used to consume us — syntax, boilerplate, wiring, test scaffolding. Our job moves inward: why, what, and how we’ll know it’s right.

    AI does most of the doing · ≈ 80%
    You decide
    ≈ 20%

    Your 20%: strategy · risk · outcome review · exceptions

    A goal, not a measurement

    After Simon Sinek’s Apple example (Start With Why, 2009)

    1. WHYthe beliefStart from a belief: challenge how things are, think differently.
    2. HOWthe processAct on it: make things well designed and easy to use.
    3. WHATthe result“We just happen to make great computers. You want to buy one?”

    In this class: humans own the why and the what; AI takes the how.

      Opening line (step 1.1, under 30 seconds):

      Today is about the things that matter when you start working seriously with AI: owning the why, knowing what you are using, giving it the right context, checking what comes back, and keeping data and decisions where they belong.

      In my words (step 1.2): Sinek’s point is that most companies explain themselves from the outside in. Here is what we make, here is how it is better, please buy it. His Apple illustration reverses the order. Apple leads with a belief, questioning how things are usually done, then explains how it acts on that belief with well-made, easy-to-use products, and only then mentions what it sells: computers. Same products, different order, and the belief is what people respond to.

      Copyright guardrail: the only verbatim Sinek wording is the one WHAT line on slide 1.2 (60 characters; Simon Sinek, How great leaders inspire action, TEDxPuget Sound, 2009). Total verbatim stays at or under 90 characters. Never show or read the full Apple passage; paraphrase only.

      The flip (step 1.3): for most of engineering history the how consumed us. Syntax, boilerplate, wiring, test scaffolding. With AI, much of the how can be delegated. Our job moves inward: why are we doing this, what exactly should exist, and how will we know it is right. We keep the acceptance criteria and the accountability.

      My working target is roughly 80% AI effort and 20% human effort, with our 20% spent on strategy, risk, outcome review and exceptions. It is a goal, not a measurement.

      Appendix F · Checked-calculation example

      Step 5.2, if asked · synthetic · not a certified design

      Synthetic off-grid sensor station: 5 W average load, 3 days of autonomy, 12 V battery usable to 50%, 3.5 peak sun hours, 70% system efficiency. Shows explicit inputs, units and an independent check.

      Daily energy = 5 W × 24 h = 120 Wh/day
Battery      = (120 × 3 Wh) / 0.5 = 720 Wh = 60 Ah at 12 V
Panel        = 120 Wh / (3.5 h × 0.7) = 49 W → choose 60 W
Recharge     = 360 Wh / (60 × 3.5 × 0.7 − 120 = 27 Wh/day) = 13.3 days

      Sanity check: 60 W × 3.5 h × 0.7 = 147 Wh/day, above the 120 Wh load. The AI computed it; an engineer decides whether 13 days to refill is acceptable.
